## What v1.2.0 is for

v1.1.0 made Jura Trace maintainable. It built a delivery mechanism that
reaches people who already have the product, restored Linux, put four months
of security fixes in front of users, made CI mean something and made the
source public. None of that changes what happens when somebody opens a file.
v1.2.0 has to. Four changes a user would notice: a Windows user stops waiting
between ninety and two hundred seconds for the Analysis Engine on every
launch, which is the worst thing about the product for roughly two thirds of
the people who install it; every download loses a 243 MiB text encoder that
exists to compute a 10 KB constant array; anyone who needs to hand their work
to somebody else can export a Verification Record, a signed, self-describing
account of what was examined and what each detector reported, rather than a
screenshot; and anyone who wants to run Jura Trace over a directory, from a
script, or on a machine with no display, gets a headless API to do it with.
Underneath those four, v1.2.0 has one job that is not a feature and cannot be
skipped: it has to be the release that the release pipeline actually
produces, from an artefact that can be rebuilt from the manifests in this
repository. v1.1.0 was assembled by hand from a macOS build whose contents
depend on what is installed on the developer's machine, and both of those are
Tier A here.

## What changed between the four documents and now

**The live legs.** Both passed, which retires the largest risk the
10 September draft carried. That draft's risk 2 read "the live updater leg
has not passed on any platform". It is now false on both, and the v1.2.0 gate
inherits two working mechanisms rather than one open question.

**A new cause under BL-SILENT-001.** Getting the Windows leg green cost five
runs and four of them went to one fault: a PowerShell array literal binds the
comma tighter than the plus, so a line built by concatenation became three
array elements, the join put newlines inside a JavaScript string literal, and
every evaluation came back `Uncaught SyntaxError`. What made it cost four runs
rather than one is the part that generalises. The helper returned a bare
`$null` for four different outcomes: could not connect, sent and heard
nothing, read forty frames and none was the reply, and the page threw. A page
that was evaluated and came back empty was recorded identically to a page that
could never be evaluated at all. That is Cause D with the sign flipped, and it
is now written into
`backlog/BL-SILENT-001-mechanisms-that-report-success-while-doing-nothing.md`.
Two rules fall out of it and they bind the quality items in this plan: a
helper returns a record saying how far it got, never a bare absence; and an
input that will be evaluated elsewhere is asserted well formed before the
expensive part of the run begins.

**Why 20 November.** Ten weeks of capacity is 35 days. The reconciled scope
is 28.25 days at the optimistic end and 39.5 at the pessimistic one, so the
optimistic and central cases fit with room and the pessimistic case is 4.5
days over. The first cut, B3, is 3 to 4 days and the second, Tier C beyond
the dependency subset, is 1.5 to 2. The overflow is covered exactly by the
top of the cut list, which is the property a date should have.

Q4-2 measures two releases and three-platform CI green by 30 November.
v1.1.0 is the first and it landed on 10 September rather than on 13 November,
so the second release has ten weeks of runway rather than the seventeen days
the v1.1.0 plan worried about. 20 November leaves seven working days before
that boundary, so a one-week slip costs schedule rather than the measure.
27 November leaves three days and no room. The count half of Q4-2 is
therefore satisfied by construction. The "three-platform CI green" half is
not, and it is not a scheduling problem; see the gate.

**The discipline that failed last time.** The v1.1.0 plan had a checkpoint and
a cut list. The checkpoint happened and was good. The cut list was never
applied, because on three occasions the date moved instead of the scope, which
is why the feature half of v1.1.0 did not ship. So: **the date does not move.
Scope absorbs everything.**

**Why B4 and A2 sit together and before B1.** They edit the same file,
`sidecar/jura-sidecar.spec`, and they are proved by the same golden-baseline
run. Capturing that baseline once and reusing it for B1 is the one ordering
benefit the packaging work has, and taking them in the other order means
capturing it twice. The v1.1.0 plan's rule, "touching model packaging in the
days before a freeze is how releases break", is honoured literally: the hard
line is that nothing in that family lands after Friday 30 October.

**Why B1 needs calendar time rather than working time.** Two to four days of
effort, spread across however long two or three 144-minute Windows CI runs
take to schedule and read, and constrained by a signing quota that allows
about nine signed Windows runs a month on the Basic tier at roughly 525
signatures a run. Starting it in week six leaves room for the second and third
run. Starting it in week eight does not.

## The cut order, and the checkpoint that applies it

**Cut order, first to go first.**

1. **B3, the Verification Record Export.** 3 to 4 days, the only Tier B item
   with no design work behind it, and the one whose own gate item is the most
   expensive: the export must be produced, its signature checked with an
   independent tool, one byte tampered and the check observed to fail. That
   implies a signing key, a documented procedure a third party can follow and
   a format stable enough to be worth checking. The name occurs nowhere in the
   tree outside the release documents and one CHANGELOG line.
2. **Tier C beyond C1 steps 1 to 5.** C2, C3 and C4, 1.5 to 2 days.
3. **B2b, the `jura` client.** The headless API stays. It is the precondition
   the rest of the adoption argument rests on, and a client that has to be
   built from source is a smaller loss than an API that does not exist.
4. **B1, Windows `--onedir`**, and only if B0 turned it into an installer
   decision rather than a port.

**B4 and A2 are not on the cut list.** Together they are 2 to 3 days, and A2
is a defect rather than a feature: a macOS build that ships 258.4 MiB of code
that CI never tested is a reproducibility fault in the artefact, and cutting
it leaves the release in the position v1.1.0 was in.

**The checkpoint is Thursday 15 October**, and it asks two questions only.
Is Tier A done. Is B2 landing inside its estimate. If either answer is no,
B3 is cut that day, not discussed.

**One earlier decision point, Friday 18 September.** B0's answer. If the MSI
cannot carry 2,645 components in one feature, B1 stops being an engineering
task and becomes the unsettled NSIS versus MSI question, which changes the
updater target at `release.yml:1780` and needs Paul rather than a commit.
Learning that in week one is the entire point of running the probe before the
window opens.

## What is explicitly out, and why

**Watermarking is v1.3.0, and the reason is the operating envelope rather
than the schedule.** BL-WM-001's robustness report, 21 March 2026, 20 images
at medium strength with an 11-byte payload, records 100 per cent survival on
original PNG, JPEG Q95 and Q85, and 0 per cent at JPEG Q70, JPEG Q60, resize
75 per cent, crop 20 per cent and screenshot simulation. There is no
intermediate band. The feature exists as a backstop for a stripped Content
Credentials manifest, and the four ways manifests get stripped in the wild are
platform recompression, screenshot, crop and download, which are the four
columns reading zero. A longer window does not move a cliff. Separately and
much cheaper: the two published web pages that claim watermarking today should
be corrected regardless, which is an afternoon and is already queued as item 6
of the site-update bundle. Shipping the feature and correcting the pages are
alternatives, not a sequence.

**Signed `jura` binaries in the release are v1.3.0.** The design's stages 5
and 6, which are packaging `jura` into the release matrix for three platforms,
signing it on macOS and Windows, plus `jura sign`, NDJSON batch output and a
Linux server tarball, are 3.5 days on top of B2b and they sit on the release
workflow, which is the part of this release with the most open faults. v1.2.0
ships the API and a client that builds with `cargo install --path cli`, which
is acceptable for an audience that is by definition comfortable with a shell.
The binary name `jura` stays reserved; the crates.io name is claimed when the
CLI first ships publicly.

**Full PDF document mode is v1.3.0**, and this is the cut most likely to draw
an argument. The honesty defect that made BL-DOC-001 urgent is closed: v1.1.0
shipped the drag-and-drop gate, `ui/src/lib/supportedFormats.ts` defines nine
image extensions and both the picker and both drop paths read it, so the
picker and the help page now agree. What remains is a feature competing on
value rather than a correction, and against the headless API it loses. It has
also become more expensive in a way BL-DOC-001 cannot know: that file is
written against c2pa 0.79.3 and lopdf 0.34, and the tree is c2pa 0.90.20 and
lopdf 0.42.0. The `pdf` feature at 0.90.20 requires lopdf 0.44.0 while
`src-tauri/Cargo.toml:93` pins 0.42 for `pdf_provenance.rs`, and those do not
unify under Cargo's 0.x caret rules, so enabling it either carries two PDF
parsers in the binary or moves `pdf_provenance.rs` in the same change.
Read-only remains the ceiling either way. And there is a claims surface: the
C2PA conforming products listing is scoped to still images, so a PDF mode
means suppressing the conformance badge on PDF results and re-scoping every
piece of copy that mentions it, in a product whose recurring defect class is
claims outrunning code.

**scikit-learn 1.9.0 is out, with a written reason this time.** `pip-audit`
reports no advisory against 1.8.0 and nothing in the product needs a 1.9.0
feature, so the entire case is that it is newer. Against that, it changes the
bytes users download, invalidates two SHA-256 constants and puts the published
AUC-ROC, false-positive and recall figures back in play, and those figures are
in the shipped application at `help/model-cards` and `help/methodology` and in
a funding application. If it is ever forced by an advisory, the six-step
evidence sequence in `deps` section 9.4 is what it needs, and the written
fallback is to stay on 1.8.0.

**torch and torchvision, pull requests #43 and #6, are not merged at all.**
They edit `sidecar/requirements-build.txt`, which by its own header is never
installed in CI or production and which `grep` shows nothing reads. So the
bump ships nothing and tests nothing, while moving the pins the production
ONNX CLIP model was exported under. The right action is the opposite of a
bump: leave the pins and add a line to that file's header saying they are the
versions the shipped model was exported under and should move only alongside a
re-export and a `scripts/validate_clip_onnx_drift.py` run.

**reqwest pull request #25 is v1.3.0 unless an advisory forces it.** 0.12.28
to 0.13.2 makes rustls the default TLS backend instead of native-tls, changes
the crypto provider from ring to aws-lc, and switches root certificates to
`rustls-platform-verifier`. That moves the application's own outbound HTTPS,
which is reverse image search and verify-from-URL, from the platform trust
store to a different one, and it fails silently on a machine behind a
TLS-inspecting proxy. Swapping the TLS backend in the release that first has
to prove Linux auto-update is the wrong order.

**winget, the Homebrew tap and AUR are decoupled from the release, not
deferred.** A winget manifest and a Homebrew cask point at release assets that
already exist, so they can be submitted the week after v1.2.0 publishes,
against v1.2.0's assets, without occupying a day of the critical path. Two to
three days of work, zero of them in the release. B4 is what makes the argument
with a reviewer comfortable: the MSI is 706 MiB today.

**Unchanged and still out:** ProofMode, the GBM v5b retrain, Conformant
signing (the blocker is an unresolved identity model, not time), ADJPEG, PRNU,
SynthID, model download on first run, quantisation, Flathub, Snap and an apt
repository.

## Risks, ranked

Each is a risk rather than a worry because there is an observation behind it.

**1. The release pipeline has never produced a release, and this plan assumes
it will.** `release.yml` has three runs and three failures. v1.1.0's macOS
disk image, updater archive, signature, checksum file and manifest were all
uploaded by hand between 18:26:59 and 18:57:51 on 10 September, and the
release was published by hand at 18:59:34, with no Actions run after
17:59:33Z. Every estimate here assumes a working pipeline at the end of it,
which is why A1 is week two and not week nine. If A1 slips, the release week
is a manual assembly again and every gate item that depends on the workflow
asserting something cannot be met.

**2. The MSI may not be able to carry the tree, and that is an installer
decision rather than an engineering task.** Tauri's bundler emits one WiX
component per resource file, all referenced from a single `MainProgram`
feature, Microsoft documents a maximum of 1,600 components per feature, and
the projected Windows tree is 2,645 files. What that produces in practice is
not established: an ICE warning, a `light.exe` failure, or a package that
builds and misbehaves at install time. Mitigation: B0 runs in week one, costs
half a day and consumes no signing quota.

**3. The macOS build ships code that CI never tested, and it is doing so
today.** `pyarrow` appears in none of the four sidecar manifests, and the
installed v1.1.0 app on this machine carries a 114 MB `pyarrow` directory and
nine `libarrow*` dylibs. `python3` here is miniconda base 3.13.5 carrying
pyarrow 23.0.1 and chromadb 0.5.23, `jura-sidecar.spec:114-119` collects
whatever `chromadb` and `sentence_transformers` it finds, and
`build-local-mac.sh:190` runs PyInstaller with that interpreter. This is the
same class as BL-DEPS-004, where the version CI tested and the version that
shipped were guaranteed to differ, except that it reached users. A2 is the
fix and it is Tier A.

**4. Windows `--onedir` touches the installer and the signing path, and
Windows has no notarisation to catch a mistake.** On macOS, signing the tree
after the bundler copies it is caught by Apple, eventually. On Windows an
installer whose entire payload is unsigned installs cleanly, launches, and
passes every assertion in `installer-test.yml` today, including on a
GitHub-hosted runner, which is not a clean consumer machine with Smart App
Control enforcing. The consequence appears on user machines weeks later,
spread across people who do not file bugs. Mitigation: sign the staged tree
before `cargo tauri bundle`, never after, and assert signatures on the
installed tree with a non-zero floor.

**5. Iteration on Windows is quota limited.** Azure Trusted Signing counts one
signature per file. At roughly 525 signatures a run and 5,000 a month on the
Basic tier, that is about nine signed Windows runs a month, shared with the
release cut itself. Which tier the account is on is not visible from this
repository and is the first thing to check before B1 starts. A late scramble
is worse rather than better, because the recovery runs are the runs the
release needs.

**6. The one file the updater downloads has no published checksum.**
`SHA256SUMS.txt` on v1.1.0 has five lines and omits `Jura.Trace.app.tar.gz`,
816.2 MiB. This is A1b and it is cheap, but it is listed as a risk for what it
indicates: the checksum file is a snapshot of whatever happened to be attached
when a step ran, and nothing asserts it covers what shipped. The same shape of
absence is what let Linux vanish from the manifest for four months.

**7. The size change can fail in a way that costs a build-matrix change.**
BL-SIZE-001 requires the precomputed embeddings to be bit-identical to runtime
ones on every platform, and onnxruntime versions differ across platforms and
the developer's host differs from CI. The documented fallback is to ship the
text encoder on the diverging platform only, which is a per-platform resources
list. Worse, `_ensure_model()` at `clip_detector.py:219-229` requires both
encoder files and returns `False` if either is missing, so shipping without
the text encoder and without the code change does not fail: the detector
becomes unavailable through one warning line and the composite is computed
from fewer detectors. Mitigation: the golden-baseline comparison in `win` 5.2,
including its floor that `clip_detect` must be true in both runs.

**8. `installer-test.yml` has never run, so the whole installer leg is
unexercised.** Zero runs, and A1c explains why it cannot run where it is. Its
install half is untested even in design.

**9. The evidence base for the Windows share is now being written to by CI.**
`updater-live-leg.yml` downloads the v1.0.0 Windows MSI on every run. It ran
ten times on 11 September and that asset's download count moved from 93 to
103, so the v1.0.0 installer totals went from 190 to 200 and the Windows share
from 63.7 to 65.5 per cent with no new user involved. The last uncontaminated
reading is 10 September: MSI 93, NSIS exe 28, DMG 42, deb 27, Windows 63.7 per
cent. Record that as the baseline and stop citing the live counter.

**10. Dependency throughput, not dependency risk.** Twenty pull requests are
open, eighteen of them bumps, and every sidecar one goes red until its lock is
regenerated, which is the guard working correctly and also makes each pip
bump a manual four-file operation. `cargo audit` reports 0 vulnerabilities and
`pip-audit` reports none against the lock, so none of this is urgent in the
security sense. Budget it as maintenance.
