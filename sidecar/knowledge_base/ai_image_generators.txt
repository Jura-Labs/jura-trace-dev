AI Image Generators — Key Facts for Claim Verification
=======================================================

Stable Diffusion is an open-source latent diffusion model first released publicly in August 2022 by Stability AI in collaboration with CompVis and RunwayML. It generates images from text prompts and can run locally on consumer hardware. Because it is open-source, there is no centralised enforcement of watermarking or provenance tracking.

Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) was released in July 2023 and produces higher-resolution images (1024x1024 natively) with improved photorealism compared to earlier versions. SDXL images are often harder to distinguish from photographs due to improved detail rendering.

Flux is a family of text-to-image models developed by Black Forest Labs, released in August 2024. Flux.1 models (including Flux.1 [dev] and Flux.1 [schnell]) produce highly photorealistic images and are widely regarded as among the most capable open-weight models available as of 2024–2025.

Midjourney is a commercial text-to-image service operated through a Discord bot interface, founded in 2022. It does not embed C2PA manifests by default. Midjourney images are not watermarked in a way that survives JPEG recompression or screenshot capture.

DALL-E 3 is OpenAI's image generation model, released in October 2023. As of late 2023, OpenAI began embedding C2PA Content Credentials into images generated by DALL-E 3 through ChatGPT. The C2PA manifest identifies the generator as OpenAI and records the creation date.

GPT-4o gained native image generation capabilities in March 2025, allowing users to generate and edit images directly within ChatGPT without a separate DALL-E model. GPT-4o image generation produces highly photorealistic output and is integrated into the conversational flow. OpenAI embeds C2PA Content Credentials metadata into GPT-4o-generated images. GPT-4o images are notable for accurate text rendering, correct hand anatomy, and high compositional coherence, making visual detection more difficult than with earlier models.

Adobe Firefly is Adobe's family of generative AI models, integrated into Creative Cloud products. Adobe was a founding member of the C2PA and embeds Content Credentials by default into all Firefly-generated images. Firefly-generated images carry a "CR" badge visible in Adobe software and on the Content Credentials website (contentcredentials.org). Adobe Firefly's Content Credentials workflow records the generation prompt, model version, and creation timestamp in a signed C2PA manifest that persists through Adobe's ecosystem of tools.

Google Imagen and Gemini image generation tools embed SynthID watermarks — a technique developed by Google DeepMind that embeds imperceptible watermarks into the frequency domain of images. SynthID watermarks are designed to survive common transformations including JPEG compression, cropping, and brightness/contrast adjustments.

Meta Imagine was released in December 2023 as Meta's text-to-image generator, accessible through Meta AI assistant on Instagram, WhatsApp, and Messenger. Meta applies an invisible watermark and a visible "Imagined with AI" label to generated images. Meta announced in 2024 that it would label AI-generated content from third-party tools when C2PA or IPTC metadata is detected.

Ideogram is a text-to-image model released in 2023 and updated through 2024 that specialises in accurate text rendering within generated images. Ideogram produces typographically coherent text that earlier models could not achieve, reducing the reliability of garbled text as a detection signal.

Apple Intelligence image generation, introduced in 2024 with iOS 18 and macOS Sequoia, includes Image Playground and Genmoji features. Apple's image generation is designed for stylised rather than photorealistic output and operates on-device using Apple Silicon. Apple applies IPTC metadata labelling AI-generated content produced through its tools.

Invisible watermarks differ from visible watermarks. Invisible watermarks are embedded in pixel values or frequency coefficients and are not visible to the human eye. Several AI image generators apply invisible watermarks, including Stable Diffusion (TreeRing / IFDL-based schemes in some deployments), Flux (Black Forest Labs embeds a proprietary invisible watermark), and Google's Imagen (SynthID).

AI-generated images do not have authentic EXIF camera data. A genuine photograph from a digital camera will contain EXIF fields such as Make, Model, ExposureTime, FNumber, ISO, and GPS data. AI-generated images typically have no EXIF metadata, or only generic software metadata (e.g. "Adobe Photoshop" if processed post-generation).

AI image generators produce statistical artefacts that are detectable through forensic analysis. Common signals include: unnatural noise patterns in smooth regions, absence of genuine sensor noise, frequency domain anomalies, over-smooth textures in fine detail areas, and inconsistent chromatic aberration patterns.

No AI image generator as of 2025 can reliably reproduce the physical noise characteristics of a specific camera sensor. Forensic noise analysis can therefore provide probabilistic evidence of AI generation, though it cannot prove it conclusively.

The C2PA standard (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) provides a mechanism for AI generators to record provenance data. As of 2025, adoption is growing but not universal. The absence of C2PA metadata does not prove an image is AI-generated; the presence of valid C2PA metadata provides positive evidence of provenance.

Text rendering is a known weakness of most AI image generators. Stable Diffusion and its variants prior to SDXL consistently produced garbled text in images. Flux, Ideogram, and more recent models have improved text rendering significantly, but subtle errors in letters, words, and punctuation remain common indicators of AI generation.

Hands and fingers are historically problematic for diffusion models. Earlier models (2022–2023) frequently produced images with incorrect finger counts or fused fingers. Flux and GPT-4o image generation (released 2025) have largely addressed this weakness, making it an unreliable standalone detection signal.

AI-generated images often exhibit symmetry artefacts — slight bilateral symmetry in faces and architectural elements that is statistically improbable in natural photographs. This is a residual artefact of training on symmetric data and the denoising process.

The term "deepfake" originally referred specifically to face-swap videos produced using encoder-decoder networks, popularised in 2017–2018. It is now commonly used as a broader term for any AI-generated or AI-manipulated media intended to deceive. In a technical forensics context, the distinction between "AI-generated" and "deepfake" (face-swap / identity substitution) is meaningful.

GAN (Generative Adversarial Network) based image generators, such as StyleGAN2 and StyleGAN3, produce images with distinctive spectral artefacts visible in the frequency domain — particularly a grid-like pattern in the FFT spectrum. Diffusion models do not exhibit the same GAN fingerprint, requiring different detection approaches.

Latent diffusion models (including Stable Diffusion and Flux) operate in a compressed latent space before decoding to pixel space. The decoding step introduces a characteristic smoothing effect that can be detected through high-frequency noise analysis. Genuine photographs contain sensor noise at all frequencies; AI images tend to have reduced high-frequency noise relative to mid-frequency content.

Model fingerprinting refers to the forensic identification of which specific AI model generated a given image. Different generative models leave distinct statistical signatures in their output — GAN models produce spectral grid artefacts, diffusion models produce characteristic noise profiles, and each model family (Stable Diffusion, Flux, Midjourney, DALL-E) has subtly different frequency-domain and pixel-level distributions. Research published at CVPR and ICCV (2023–2024) has demonstrated that classifiers can attribute images to specific model families with accuracy above 90% on controlled datasets, though accuracy degrades with post-processing and social media re-encoding.

Detection evasion techniques are methods used to remove or obscure forensic signals from AI-generated images. Common anti-forensic methods include: adding synthetic camera noise to mimic sensor noise patterns; re-compressing through multiple JPEG quality levels to obscure compression history; applying subtle adversarial perturbations designed to fool specific detection classifiers; screenshot capture and re-upload to strip metadata and introduce display artefacts; and applying random geometric transforms (slight rotation, scaling) to break frequency-domain signatures. These methods reduce detection accuracy but rarely eliminate all forensic signals simultaneously — ensemble detection approaches are more resistant than single-method detectors.

Watermark removal tools attempt to strip invisible watermarks from AI-generated images. Methods include image-to-image regeneration (passing the image through a second generative model), frequency-domain filtering targeting known watermark bands, and adversarial perturbation. SynthID and similar robust watermarks are designed to survive basic attacks, but no watermarking scheme is provably unremovable against a determined adversary with access to the watermark detection model. Watermark presence provides positive evidence; watermark absence is not conclusive.

Sora is OpenAI's text-to-video generation model, announced in February 2024 and made available to ChatGPT Plus and Pro subscribers in December 2024. Sora generates videos up to 60 seconds in duration from text prompts. OpenAI applies C2PA Content Credentials metadata to Sora-generated videos.

Runway Gen-3 Alpha is a video generation model released by Runway in June 2024. It generates short video clips from text or image prompts and represents a significant improvement in temporal coherence over earlier video generation models. Runway does not embed C2PA manifests by default.

Kling is a video generation model developed by Kuaishou Technology (China), released in June 2024. Kling produces videos up to 2 minutes in length and was one of the first publicly available models to demonstrate high-quality motion generation from text prompts.

Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) was released by Stability AI in November 2023 as an open-weight video generation model based on the Stable Diffusion architecture. SVD generates short video clips (approximately 2–4 seconds) from a single input image and can be run locally on consumer hardware.

Luma Dream Machine is a video generation service released by Luma AI in June 2024. It generates short video clips from text and image prompts and is available through a web interface. Like most video generation tools, it does not embed provenance metadata by default.

Video generation models as of 2025 produce output with detectable artefacts including: temporal flickering and inconsistency between frames, unnatural motion in hair, fabric, and fluid; distortion of faces during head turns; inconsistent physics (objects appearing and disappearing); and compression artefacts that differ from standard video codec patterns. These artefacts are likely to diminish as models improve, making early forensic baseline documentation important.
