Digital Rights and Cultural Heritage — Content Protection and Provenance
=========================================================================

Cultural heritage digitisation involves creating high-resolution digital reproductions of physical artefacts, artworks, manuscripts, photographs, and archival documents held by galleries, libraries, archives, and museums (collectively known as GLAM institutions). Digitisation enables wider access to collections but creates digital assets that can be copied, modified, and redistributed without authorisation, raising significant content protection challenges.

Orphan works are copyrighted works whose rights holders cannot be identified or located after a diligent search. Orphan works represent a substantial portion of many cultural heritage collections — estimates suggest 40–70% of archival photograph collections consist of orphan works. The UK orphan works licensing scheme, administered by the Intellectual Property Office (IPO), allows use of orphan works after a documented diligent search. The EU Orphan Works Directive (2012/28/EU) permits digitisation and online access by cultural institutions under similar conditions. Provenance metadata (including C2PA) can help document the chain of custody for orphan works.

Creative Commons licensing provides a standardised framework for rights holders to grant public permissions for use of their work. The six standard CC licences (CC BY, CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC, CC BY-NC-SA, CC BY-ND, CC BY-NC-ND) specify different combinations of attribution, share-alike, non-commercial, and no-derivatives conditions. CC0 is a public domain dedication waiving all copyright. Creative Commons metadata can be embedded in IPTC and XMP fields and referenced in C2PA assertions, creating a machine-readable rights chain.

UK copyright law grants copyright automatically to the creator of an original work, including photographs, without registration. Copyright in photographs lasts for the life of the photographer plus 70 years. The UK recognises "database right" (sui generis right) protecting substantial investment in compiling databases, relevant to digitised collection metadata. Crown copyright applies to works created by UK government bodies and lasts 125 years from creation. The Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 (CDPA) is the primary legislation.

The EU Digital Single Market Directive (2019/790) includes provisions relevant to cultural heritage. Article 3 permits text and data mining (TDM) for scientific research purposes. Article 4 permits TDM for any purpose unless the rights holder expressly reserves this right. Article 6 permits cultural heritage institutions to make copies of works in their permanent collections for preservation. These provisions affect how digitised collections interact with AI training — institutions can opt out of TDM by rights holders by machine-readable reservation of rights in metadata.

GLAM institutions face specific content protection challenges including: preventing unauthorised commercial use of high-resolution digitised images; tracking the provenance of images as they spread across the web; asserting institutional rights over digitised reproductions; managing rights for donor and depositor collections with complex ownership histories; and balancing open access goals with revenue generation from image licensing.

Institutional watermarking practices in GLAM contexts serve both identification and deterrence purposes. Visible watermarks (logos, text overlays) deter casual misuse but degrade the image for legitimate users. Invisible watermarks (frequency-domain embedding) allow post-hoc identification without visual degradation. Many institutions use a tiered approach: low-resolution images with visible watermarks for browsing, high-resolution clean images available through licensing, and invisible watermarks embedded in all distributed copies for tracking.

Rights metadata standards used in cultural heritage include: IPTC (International Press Telecommunications Council) — fields for creator, copyright notice, usage rights, source; XMP (Extensible Metadata Platform) — Adobe's metadata framework embedded in files, supporting IPTC Core and Dublin Core schemas; Dublin Core — 15 core metadata elements (title, creator, subject, description, publisher, date, rights, etc.) widely used in library and archive cataloguing; and METS (Metadata Encoding and Transmission Standard) — used for packaging complex digital objects with structural and administrative metadata.

Image rights management in museum contexts involves tracking reproduction rights, loan agreements, donor restrictions, and cultural sensitivity considerations. Many museums operate image licensing programmes (e.g. Bridgeman Images, Art Resource) that generate revenue from commercial use of collection images. The legal basis for charging for reproductions of public domain works is debated — the 2014 UK case Bridgeman Art Library v. Corel Corp (decided under US law) held that faithful reproductions of public domain artworks lack sufficient originality for copyright, but UK law may differ, and the question remains unsettled.

Provenance as a concept bridges the physical and digital worlds. In the archival and museum sense, provenance (or "provenience") refers to the documented chain of ownership and custody of a physical object — essential for establishing authenticity, legal ownership, and cultural significance. In the digital sense, provenance refers to the recorded history of a digital file — who created it, how it was modified, and how it was distributed. C2PA digital provenance draws conceptually from archival provenance practice: both aim to establish an unbroken, verifiable chain of custody that supports trust in the object's authenticity.

The UK Public Lending Right and Artist's Resale Right (droit de suite) are relevant to digital distribution of cultural content. Artist's Resale Right entitles visual artists (and their estates for 70 years post-mortem) to a royalty on resales of their original artworks above a threshold price. As cultural heritage content moves online, the intersection of resale rights with digital reproduction licensing becomes increasingly relevant.

Open access policies in cultural heritage: a growing number of institutions have adopted open access policies for digitised collections, making high-resolution images freely available under Creative Commons licences or public domain dedications. Notable examples include the Metropolitan Museum of Art (CC0 since 2017), Rijksmuseum (CC0), the Smithsonian Institution (Open Access since 2020), and the British Library (various CC licences). Open access does not eliminate the need for provenance tracking — institutions still need to track usage, maintain attribution, and verify the integrity of their distributed images.

AI training and cultural heritage collections: the use of digitised cultural heritage images in AI training datasets has raised significant concerns. Many collections were digitised and published under terms that did not anticipate AI training use. The EU DSM Directive's TDM exceptions (Articles 3–4) provide a legal framework, but institutions must actively opt out of commercial TDM by embedding machine-readable rights reservations. The UK government's proposed TDM exception (consulted on in 2022, not enacted as of early 2025) would have permitted broader TDM, raising concerns among cultural institutions about loss of control over their digitised collections.

Digital preservation and format migration: cultural heritage digital assets require long-term preservation strategies. Format migration (converting between file formats as technology evolves) can strip or corrupt embedded metadata including provenance information. GLAM institutions must ensure that provenance metadata survives format migration. C2PA manifests are format-specific and may need to be re-signed after format conversion. Archival formats such as TIFF and PNG are preferred for long-term preservation; lossy formats such as JPEG are used for access copies.

The International Image Interoperability Framework (IIIF) is a set of open standards for delivering high-resolution images over the web, widely adopted by cultural heritage institutions. IIIF Presentation API and Image API enable institutions to serve images with associated metadata. IIIF does not currently integrate C2PA provenance, but the combination of IIIF delivery with C2PA-signed source images could provide both interoperability and provenance verification.

Cultural sensitivity and indigenous cultural heritage: some digitised cultural heritage content is subject to cultural protocols that restrict viewing, reproduction, or distribution — particularly indigenous cultural materials. Traditional Knowledge (TK) Labels and Notices, developed by Local Contexts, provide culturally appropriate metadata labels that can be attached to digital objects. These labels complement legal rights metadata and can be referenced alongside C2PA assertions to encode cultural use restrictions.
