Video Forensic Techniques — Detection and Verification
========================================================

Video deepfake detection operates at two levels: per-frame analysis, which applies image-level forensic techniques to individual video frames, and temporal analysis, which examines consistency and patterns across sequences of frames. Both levels are necessary because per-frame analysis alone misses artefacts that manifest only in motion, while temporal analysis alone may miss single-frame manipulations.

Per-frame deepfake analysis applies image forensic detectors — including noise analysis, ELA, frequency spectrum analysis, and neural network classifiers — to sampled frames extracted from the video. Sampling strategies vary by investigation depth: standard analysis may examine 6 evenly-spaced frames, deep analysis 20 frames, and archival analysis 40 or more frames. Per-frame scores are aggregated using weighted formulas (e.g. 0.5 x mean + 0.3 x max + 0.2 x temporal signal) to produce an overall detection score.

Face-swap detection in video targets boundary artefacts where the swapped face meets the original head and neck. Common signals include: visible blending seams at the jawline and hairline; inconsistent skin texture and pore detail between the face region and surrounding skin; lighting discontinuities where the face is lit from a different direction than the body; mismatched skin tone or colour temperature between face and neck; and unnatural eye reflections that do not match the scene's light sources. Modern face-swap tools (DeepFaceLab, FaceSwap) have improved blending but still produce detectable artefacts under close examination.

Lip-sync deepfake detection targets face-reenactment manipulations where a person's mouth movements are altered to match different audio. Detection methods include: audio-visual synchronisation analysis comparing phoneme timing with lip shapes; temporal consistency of lip motion checking for unnatural acceleration or jitter; texture analysis of the mouth region detecting blending artefacts; and comparison of lip movement dynamics against known speech patterns. The wav2lip model (2020) and its successors produce increasingly convincing lip-sync but leave temporal and texture artefacts detectable by specialised classifiers.

Temporal consistency signals measure how forensic properties change across sequential frames. In authentic video, noise patterns, spectral characteristics, and texture features change smoothly according to the physics of the scene. In manipulated video, these properties may exhibit abrupt shifts or drift patterns inconsistent with natural scene changes. Key temporal signals include: noise drift (frame-to-frame variation in noise level statistics), spectral drift (changes in frequency-domain energy distribution), and LBP drift (local binary pattern texture descriptor variation).

Video compression significantly affects forensic analysis. Standard video codecs (H.264, H.265/HEVC, VP9, AV1) use inter-frame prediction, motion compensation, and aggressive quantisation that alter pixel-level forensic signals. Keyframes (I-frames) retain more forensic detail than predicted frames (P-frames, B-frames). Forensic analysis should preferentially sample keyframes when possible. Multiple re-encodings compound the loss of forensic signal, making detection progressively harder.

Social media video re-encoding: all major social media platforms re-encode uploaded videos, which degrades forensic signals. X (Twitter) re-encodes to H.264 at reduced bitrate. TikTok applies heavy re-encoding with resolution scaling and bitrate reduction. Instagram Reels re-encodes with resolution capping and bitrate normalisation. YouTube preserves higher quality but still re-encodes with its own codec settings. Each platform's re-encoding pipeline introduces its own compression artefacts that may mask or mimic manipulation signals. Forensic analysis of social-media-sourced video must account for platform-specific degradation.

Frame rate anomalies can serve as forensic signals. Authentic video recorded by a camera has a consistent frame rate (24, 25, 30, or 60 fps). AI-generated video may have irregular frame timing, duplicated frames, or frame rates that do not match standard camera recording rates. Some deepfake pipelines operate at lower frame rates (e.g. 15 fps) and then interpolate to the target frame rate, introducing temporal smoothness artefacts that are absent in genuine high-frame-rate capture.

Audio-visual synchronisation analysis compares the audio track with the visual content to detect mismatches. In authentic video, lip movements correspond precisely to speech audio with consistent latency. Deepfake face-reenactment and audio dubbing introduce synchronisation errors — the lips may lead or lag the audio by variable amounts. Automated detection measures phoneme-viseme alignment at sub-frame precision to identify these discrepancies.

Known video deepfake tools as of 2025 include: DeepFaceLab (open-source face swap, the most widely used deepfake tool); FaceSwap (open-source face swap); wav2lip (lip-sync generation from audio); Sora (OpenAI text-to-video, generating up to 60-second clips); Runway Gen-3 Alpha (text/image-to-video); Kling (Kuaishou, text-to-video up to 2 minutes); Luma Dream Machine (text/image-to-video); and Stable Video Diffusion (open-weight image-to-video). Face-swap tools produce different artefact profiles than text-to-video generators and require different detection approaches.

Noise drift is a temporal forensic signal that measures frame-to-frame variation in noise level statistics (mean, standard deviation, kurtosis of the noise residual). In authentic video, noise varies smoothly with scene content and exposure. In face-swap deepfakes, the noise profile of the face region may differ from the background, and this difference varies inconsistently across frames as the face-swap model processes each frame independently.

Spectral drift measures changes in the frequency-domain energy distribution across frames. Authentic video shows smooth spectral evolution as scene content changes. Deepfake face regions may exhibit spectral properties that do not evolve consistently with the background, particularly in the high-frequency bands where generator artefacts concentrate.

LBP (Local Binary Pattern) drift measures frame-to-frame variation in texture descriptor statistics. LBP captures micro-texture patterns that are sensitive to the statistical properties of the image generation process. In deepfake video, the LBP distribution of the manipulated region may shift differently from the unmanipulated regions across frames.

Eye reflection analysis is a targeted forensic technique for face-swap detection. In authentic photographs and video, specular reflections in both eyes should show the same light sources in consistent geometric positions. Deepfake face swaps frequently produce inconsistent eye reflections — different shapes, positions, or numbers of light sources in each eye — because the generator does not model the physical optics of reflection. This signal is most reliable in high-resolution video with visible catchlights.

Video provenance using C2PA: the C2PA specification supports signing of video files in MP4 and MOV (QuickTime) container formats. The C2PA manifest is stored within the BMFF (Base Media File Format) container structure as a JUMBF box. Video C2PA signing records the same provenance information as image signing — creation tool, editing history, ingredient references — but must also handle the time-based nature of video content. C2PA for video is supported by the c2pa-rs library used by Jura Trace.

Biological signal analysis in video examines physiological patterns that deepfake generators fail to reproduce. Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) measures subtle colour changes in facial skin caused by blood flow — authentic video contains a periodic pulse signal that deepfakes lack or reproduce incorrectly. Blinking pattern analysis checks for natural blink rate (15–20 blinks per minute) and realistic blink dynamics. Micro-expression analysis examines involuntary facial movements that are difficult for generators to synthesise consistently.

Motion estimation forensics analyses optical flow patterns between frames. Authentic video shows physically plausible motion trajectories consistent with rigid body dynamics and natural human movement. Deepfake video may show motion artefacts including: jitter in regions near face boundaries, temporally inconsistent head pose relative to body movement, and motion discontinuities at the edges of manipulated regions.

Video authentication best practice follows the same multi-signal principle as image forensics: no single technique is sufficient. A robust video forensic pipeline combines per-frame analysis, temporal consistency signals, audio-visual synchronisation, compression analysis, metadata verification, and where available, C2PA provenance verification. The confidence of the overall assessment increases with the number of independent signals that agree.
